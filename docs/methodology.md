# How this research works

This IS117005 portfolio asks: **Which careers help people build, understand, and responsibly use AI—and what evidence supports their growth?** It combines occupational projections with descriptions of emerging work, then turns those findings into practical exploration projects.

## Scope and review date

Research reviewed **November 5, 2026**. Numerical employment projections describe the **United States**; global employer surveys are identified separately. The site focuses on technology and adjacent product, security, and governance work. It is a selected field guide, not a complete ranking of every AI career.

## Two kinds of career evidence

| Evidence type | What it tells us | What it cannot establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Established occupation | BLS measures a broad occupation and projects employment over a stated decade. | A growth rate for an AI specialization within that occupation. |
| Emerging title | Employer descriptions and professional frameworks show the work and skills associated with a title. | A comparable national employment count or a reliable title-specific growth rate. |

The explorer applies the label **Established occupation** only when its role aligns with the cited BLS occupation. Emerging roles display **No separate forecast**. Similar work can have different titles across employers, and one title can cover very different responsibilities.

## Source selection

1. Use primary sources: BLS occupational data, original employer research, official professional frameworks, and employers' descriptions of their work.
2. Read the source itself. Search result snippets can refer to an older projection period.
3. Keep the geography, date, and projection period attached to each growth claim.
4. Separate source findings from this portfolio's suggested skills, projects, and career routes.
5. Link claims close to the relevant passage and collect the references on the [sources page](/sources).

The learning plans and project briefs are original suggestions. They do not represent completed student projects, required credentials, or guaranteed hiring routes.

## Reading growth correctly

Percentage growth measures the change in employment between two years. It is different from the number of new jobs and from annual openings, which may also include replacement needs. A smaller occupation can have a high growth rate and fewer opportunities than a larger occupation growing more slowly.

National projections do not describe every local job market. Employer expectations are survey findings, not government forecasts. None of these measures guarantees an individual job offer. See the [BLS explanation of employment projections](https://www.bls.gov/emp/documentation/projections-methods.htm).

## Keeping the portfolio current

For each future review, record the date, inspect the original sources for a new projection period, and update the comparison table and role data together. Recheck emerging role descriptions when employer pages change. Preserve older periods only when clearly labeled as historical.
